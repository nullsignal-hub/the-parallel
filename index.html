The Parallel

A whole mind was cut into 166,691 pieces and published.
One of them is yours.

scroll
I  ·  The cut

They sliced a brain until nothing was left of it but numbers.

Then they gave the numbers away.

Every neuron of one male Drosophila was traced through electron micrographs until the whole nervous system existed as a list: 166,691 cells, 11,691 kinds of cell, about 125 million connections between them. The animal is long dead. The map is not.

What you are about to see is not an illustration of a neuron. It is a neuron — the coordinates of one real cell, pulled from a public bucket while this page loads, and drawn.

This cell is the reason the fly can be startled. There are two of them in the whole animal, one per side, and they are the thickest wires in the brain. They are used for about five milliseconds at a time.

II  ·  The copies

The map has been downloaded onto thousands of machines.

Within two weeks of publication, people had this brain flying a plane, playing Minecraft, trading stocks, writing haiku. Every one of those is the same nervous system, started again from the same first instant, given a different world to live in.

Derek Parfit asked what happens if you are copied exactly — which one is you. He had to imagine the machine. Here the machine exists, the copies are running, and the answer is still no clearer than it was.

A copy that has begun to diverge is not a copy any more. It is a life, running in parallel with yours, that you happen to have started.

III  ·  The fission

And then there is the strange thing in the data itself.

Of the 166,691 cells, 75,215 sit on the left and 75,119 sit on the right. For almost every cell on one side there is a near-identical cell on the other: same type, same job, mirrored geometry. Two copies, built from the same instructions, wired into the same skull, looking at two different halves of the world.

Parfit's hardest case was fission — split a person in two and both halves have an equal claim to being them. He meant it as a thought experiment.

In this brain it is a measurement.

The same cell type, twice, in one animal. Neither is the original. Neither is the copy.

IV  ·  The ones with no other side

392 cells sit exactly on the midline.

They have no mirror. Nothing was duplicated when they were made. Whatever they do, they do it once, for the whole animal, and there is no parallel version of them anywhere in this nervous system.

You may turn out to be one of those.

V  ·  Which one are you

Ten questions. Or none at all.

Your answers are not scored against a personality theory. They are scored against the actual anatomy: where a cell sits between sensing and acting, how far its wire reaches, how many copies of it exist, how many synapses it carries, how much it branches. Those five numbers — act, reach, population, load, tangle — are measured from the data for every cell below. Your ten answers, or the shape of your own hand moving, get converted into the same five numbers, and the cell whose numbers sit closest to yours is the one you get.

One caveat, stated plainly: you are matched against a shortlist of — named, well-studied cells, not against all 166,691. Every cell in the shortlist is a real individual with a real bodyId — the dataset's own catalog number for that one cell, the same number you'll see printed on your result and, if you order a print, on the poster itself — and every number you are shown is measured. But a shortlist is a shortlist.
